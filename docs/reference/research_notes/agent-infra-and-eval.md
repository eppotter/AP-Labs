## EVALUATION: what state-of-the-art agent evaluation actually looks like in 2026, and the honest consensus on LLM-as-judge

### Takeaway
The practitioner consensus in 2026 is *not* "LLM-as-judge is the eval method" — it is "error analysis first, deterministic code assertions wherever a rule can decide, and a narrow, individually-validated LLM judge only for genuinely subjective failures." The 2026 academic literature has moved the critique from "judges are biased" to the sharper and more damaging "judges can be highly *reliable* while being *invalid*," which means vendor dashboards showing stable judge scores are not evidence of measurement quality.

### Cited Findings

**The practitioner playbook (the most authoritative non-vendor source found):**
- Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's evals FAQ (updated 2026-09-18) states the rule plainly: use a code-based eval "when a deterministic rule can identify the failure," and "use an LLM judge when the failure requires human judgment." It explicitly advises favoring assertions over LLM-as-judge evaluators — [hamel.dev evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) `[FETCHED]`
- Same source, on judge cost and maintenance burden: LLM-as-judge evaluators "require 100+ labeled examples, ongoing weekly maintenance," so they should be reserved for failures you will iterate on repeatedly — [hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) `[FETCHED]`
- Same source, on judge architecture: use "a separate judge for each type of failure"; a common failure mode is one judge asked to "catch too many different kinds of errors at once" — [hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) `[FETCHED]`
- Judge validation protocol, concretely specified: split labeled data train/dev/test at roughly 10–20% / 40–45% / 40–45%; aim for "30 to 50 Pass examples and 30 to 50 Fail examples" in both dev and test; label "100 to 200 examples for each failure mode"; track **both** TPR (recall on real failures) and TNR (not flagging good outputs), because "catching more failures can come at the cost of more false alarms" — [hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) `[FETCHED]`
- Golden/regression set guidance: the repeatable eval set "often grows to 100 or more examples" and should "remain cheap enough to run often"; run it when you change the application; and critically — "If everything keeps passing, this is a sign that the eval is less useful," so retire or downgrade stale evals — [hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) `[FETCHED]`
- Online/production sampling: use random + clustering + data analysis + classification + user feedback, and "keep some random traces in every batch," because feedback "misses problems that users do not report." Review cadence: 100+ fresh traces per cycle every 2–4 weeks, 10–20 weekly in between — [hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) `[FETCHED]`
- Human review loop design: one domain expert as "benevolent dictator" for small/medium teams; with multiple annotators, label independently *before* discussion, measure Cohen's Kappa, then run alignment sessions and update the rubric — [hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) `[FETCHED]`
- Direct criticism of the vendor category from the same source: "Many eval vendors promote off the shelf metrics, which ensnare engineers into superfluous tasks," and "Generic evaluations waste time and create false confidence when you use them as quality measures." It also answers "Generally no" to eval-driven development, advising you write "evaluators for errors you discover, not errors you imagine" — [hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) `[FETCHED]`

**The 2026 academic critique — reliability vs. validity:**
- A large-scale preprint evaluating 21 judges across ~541,000 judgments reports raw exact-match agreement exceeding chance-corrected Cohen's κ by 33–41 points on MT-Bench, which the authors term "kappa deflation," arguing that validation built on raw agreement is misleading — [arXiv 2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19544v1) `[SNIPPET]`
- Same paper: two production-deployed judges showed test–retest reliability above 0.95 *while also* showing severe position bias, and judge-produced rankings shifted by up to 14 positions across benchmarks — [arXiv 2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19544v1) `[SNIPPET]`
- RAND's "Judge Reliability Harness" (March 2026) tested four judges and concluded none was uniformly reliable across benchmarks; JudgeBiasBench reportedly shows frontier models exceeding 50% error rates on hard bias tests — [arXiv 2603.05399](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.05399); summarized in [Adaline](https://www.adaline.ai/blog/llm-as-a-judge-reliability-bias) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- The human ground truth is also unstable: a secondary analysis reports expert and crowd pools splitting on 23.6% of items both pools independently called unanimous, and cites a January 2026 psychiatrist rating study with inter-rater ICC between 0.087 and 0.295 — [Kili Technology](https://kili-technology.com/blog/data-story-who-judges-the-judge-llm-as-a-judge) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- A 2026 preprint argues the open problem is *construct validity*, not reliability — i.e. whether a judge is measuring the thing that changed — [arXiv 2608.24419](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.24419) `[SNIPPET]`

**What practitioners actually do (CHI '26 interview study — the single best non-vendor evidence on practice):**
- "Results-Actionability Gap: Understanding How Practitioners Evaluate LLM Products in the Wild" interviews 19 practitioners across sectors, identifies ten evaluation practices "ranging from informal 'vibe checks' to organizational meta-work," confirms four previously documented challenges and adds a fifth — [arXiv 2604.16304](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16304) `[FETCHED abstract]`
- The named contribution: practitioners *collect* evaluation data but cannot convert findings into concrete improvements. The authors frame ad-hoc interpretive practices as "reasonable adaptations to how LLMs behave, not simply methodological failures," and argue the research opportunity is "systematizing emerging practices rather than inventing new evaluation frameworks" — [arXiv 2604.16304](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16304) `[FETCHED abstract]`

### Inferences
- The market has bifurcated: **Braintrust** sells the eval workflow, **Langfuse/Phoenix** sell OTel-native tracing you can self-host, **LangSmith** sells the LangChain-native path, and **Galileo** is the one trying to straddle eval and runtime enforcement. Nobody sells the thing the CHI '26 paper says is actually missing — the bridge from eval result to a concrete fix.
- The "reliability without validity" result is the single most important finding for anyone buying an eval platform in 2026: a platform that shows you a *stable* judge score is giving you no evidence that the score is valid. None of the platform marketing surfaced addresses construct validity.
- Hamel/Shankar's position is best read as *not* an argument against LLM-as-judge but an argument for **proportionality**: deterministic first, judge only where human judgment is irreducible, and judge only after TPR/TNR validation against human labels. That is a materially stricter standard than any vendor default.
- The honest-consensus answer: LLM-as-judge is usable but only as a *validated instrument*, and the validation cost (100–200 labeled examples per failure mode plus weekly upkeep) is what most teams skip — which is exactly what makes most deployed judges untrustworthy.

## GUARDRAILS: how runtime guardrail products enforce data-exposure policy, and documented failure modes

### Takeaway
Shipping guardrail products are overwhelmingly **classifier-based or prompt/flow-based**, not deterministic, and the research consensus is that this is the wrong layer for consequential enforcement: classifiers are probabilistic, and documented attacks flip their verdicts without changing what the protected model reads. The pattern the literature converges on is "deterministic controls **enforce**, classifiers **detect**," with the actual enforcement pushed down to the tool/database/semantic layer — which means the column-level policy case ("this column must never reach this audience") is, in practice, not solved by any guardrail product but by database/semantic-layer grants.

### Cited Findings

**Mechanisms, product by product:**
- **NVIDIA NeMo Guardrails** is a proxy between user and LLM, with rails authored in **Colang**, "a formal modeling language for specifying flows of events." Crucially, flow matching is where nondeterminism enters: Colang's user canonical forms are "indexed in a vector database to allow for efficient nearest-neighbors lookup." One critical paper argues this makes the architecture "inherently probabilistic"; other 2026 sources describe it as "a deterministic policy executor once its flows are configured." **Both are true of different parts of the system** — authored flows are deterministic, intent matching is a vector lookup — [NeMo Guardrails paper, arXiv 2310.10501](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.10501); [arXiv 2604.01483](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01483v1) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Guardrails AI** is validator/schema-based: a RAIL spec, a Guard object wrapping the LLM, and declarative validators enforcing type and factuality constraints with streaming repair. Assessment from a critical source: "syntactic validation is fast and deterministic" but "logically shallow" — it checks structure, not policy — [arXiv 2604.01483](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01483v1) `[SNIPPET]` `[source is advocating its own Lean 4 alternative — treat as advocacy]`
- **Amazon Bedrock Guardrails** sensitive-information filtering is self-described as "a probabilistic machine learning (ML) based solution." Its custom regex filter "does not support a regex lookaround match." Documented leak: the trace output's `match` field "contains the original PII value, not the masked output" — i.e. the guardrail's own telemetry can leak what it redacted — [AWS Bedrock docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-sensitive-filters.html) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Datadog** describes output guardrails as "regex scrubbing to redact leaked secrets (e.g., API keys) before returning responses" — regex, on the output path — [Datadog](https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/llm-guardrails-best-practices/) `[SNIPPET]`
- **LangChain** ships a first-party guardrails module in its OSS docs, placing guards at the SDK level inside the application — [LangChain guardrails docs](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langchain/guardrails) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Lakera** — acquired by **Check Point**, announced September 2025, closed **2025-10-22**. Reported deal value ~$300M in press coverage, but Check Point's own securities filing put total consideration at ~**$201.8M**; prefer the filing. Lakera Guard, Lakera Red and Gandalf are being folded into Check Point's Infinity AI security platform; the lakera.ai brand was still live as of late July 2026 — [BankInfoSecurity](https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/check-point-adds-ai-application-defense-lakera-purchase-a-29460); [Calcalist](https://www.calcalistech.com/ctechnews/article/rj5bc1vige) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Protect AI** — acquired by **Palo Alto Networks**, completed July 2025, folded into **Prisma AIRS** — [guptadeepak roundup](https://guptadeepak.com/tools/top-5-ai-threat-detection-tools-2026/) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Credal** — I could not find documentation of column-level policy enforcement for LLM queries. Credal's own security page mentions "schema level access policies to further guard against even the risk of Credal-generated code problems" (code-gen risk, not audience-scoped column filtering) and zero-data-retention agreements with OpenAI/Anthropic/Cohere — [credal.ai/security](https://credal.ai/security) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`

**Documented bypass and failure modes:**
- **EchoGram (HiddenLayer):** short "flip token" sequences can change a guardrail classifier's verdict *without changing what the protected model reads* — the cleanest documented demonstration that classifier guardrails are not a security boundary — [nhimg guardrails guide](https://nhimg.org/ai-guardrails-and-ai-firewalls-guide) `[SNIPPET]`
- Standard evasion set: encoding, translation, and splitting instructions across turns defeat classifier guardrails; "adaptive attacks bypass static detectors at high rates" — [securetom](https://securetom.com/blog/llm-guardrail-bypass-classifier-judge-testing); [nhimg](https://nhimg.org/ai-guardrails-and-ai-firewalls-guide) `[SNIPPET]`
- Regex-specific limits: regex PII detection "catches obvious formats and misses plenty" and should be treated as "a reduction in exposure, not a guarantee." Presidio-style entity analysis catches "emails, names, addresses, the stuff regexes miss" — [dev.to TypeScript guardrails guide](https://dev.to/gabrielanhaia/guardrails-in-typescript-input-validation-output-filters-refusal-paths-5eap) `[SNIPPET]`
- Ordering failure mode: "redaction must run before anything that logs" — otherwise the guardrail is bypassed by your own observability stack — [dev.to](https://dev.to/gabrielanhaia/guardrails-in-typescript-input-validation-output-filters-refusal-paths-5eap) `[SNIPPET]`
- Architectural critique of the whole product category: specialist guards "intercept calls at SDK level inside the application, not at a traffic chokepoint with routing and RBAC," and Langfuse's own docs "delegate runtime security to third-party libraries," positioning the product as ex-post evaluation — [laminar.sh](https://laminar.sh/article/langfuse-alternatives-2026) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`

**The column-level data-exposure case specifically:**
- **Decoding-time deterministic enforcement exists in research, not product.** PCC-SQL applies a **per-token logits mask so disallowed columns cannot be generated**, with the policy defined over three *roles* (output, filter condition, aggregation argument) — the authors argue that refusing based on which columns a query merely mentions is too coarse "because compliance depends on how a column is used." Reported **0% leakage on Spider-CU with coverage up to 88.7%** (authors' own benchmark, not independently verified) — [arXiv 2607.12341](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.12341) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Semantic layer as the enforcement point** is the dominant vendor position: Dremio describes masking UDFs embedded in view definitions "so raw values never reach unauthorized identities"; Databricks argues for policies attached to metric definitions so agents inherit them — [Dremio](https://www.dremio.com/blog/semantic-layer-governance-control-what-ai-agents-access/); [Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/blog/semantic-layer-architecture-components-design-patterns-and-ai-integration) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Layered practice from an OSS implementation:** an Azure NL2SQL project stacks SQL-text checks (single statement, SELECT-only, allow-listed columns, star-expansion checked against policy) *and* database-level column DENY, "so the engine refuses what the guardrails miss" — [GitHub sharath568/azure-data-nl2sql](https://github.com/sharath568/azure-data-nl2sql) `[SNIPPET]`
- Quantified governed-vs-ungoverned: one preprint reports governed arms submitted **zero** mutating statements while ungoverned arms submitted **166 across 26 tasks** — [arXiv 2609.22259](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.22259) `[SNIPPET]`
- Formal verification is being proposed as the alternative: "Type-Checked Compliance: Deterministic Guardrails for Agentic Financial Systems Using Lean 4 Theorem Proving," and a separate theory paper "What Can Be Enforced? A Theory of Certified Runtime Safety for Tool-Using Agents" — [arXiv 2604.01483](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01483v1); [arXiv 2607.22868](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.22868) `[SNIPPET]`

### Inferences
- **No shipping guardrail product found enforces "this column must never reach this audience" deterministically.** The products do classifier-based detection (Lakera/Check Point, Prisma AIRS, Galileo), flow-based intent matching with a vector lookup (NeMo), or schema/regex validation (Guardrails AI, Bedrock, Datadog). Deterministic column-level enforcement exists only in (a) the database/semantic layer, which is not an AI product, and (b) research prototypes like PCC-SQL.
- Given EchoGram, any architecture that relies on a classifier as the *last* line of defense before a data egress is not defensible. The defensible designs put a deterministic check at the point of side effect — the tool layer, the DB grant, or the decoder.
- The Bedrock trace-leak and the "redact before you log" failure mode are the same bug class: the guardrail is correct on the user-facing path and bypassed on the telemetry path. This is a strong argument for enforcement at the data layer rather than the response layer.
- "Prompt-based, classifier-based, or deterministic?" The honest answer for 2026 products is: **mostly classifier, with a deterministic veneer (schema/regex) and a prompt-based fallback.** The marketing language ("policy," "guardrail," "firewall") systematically overstates determinism.

### Gaps
- No single authoritative benchmark comparing bypass rates across classifier vs. deterministic guardrail approaches exists. Multiple sources said so independently.
- I could not find Credal product documentation describing audience-scoped column-level filtering; this needs a direct docs read or a vendor conversation.
- I found no public bypass/failure-mode disclosures for Galileo's runtime guardrails specifically.
- Post-acquisition product reality for Lakera (inside Check Point Infinity) and Protect AI (inside Prisma AIRS) is unclear — whether the standalone detection APIs survive is not documented in what I retrieved.

## PROMPT INJECTION: state of practice for untrusted agent input, including the attacker-controlled PR case

### Takeaway
The attacker-controlled-PR case is no longer theoretical — it is a **CVE with a documented full exploit chain** against Anthropic's own `claude-code-action` (CVE-2025-66032), and the structural insight is that the attacker's required privilege dropped from "repo write access" to "a free GitHub account that can open an issue or PR." Rigorous threat models exist, but they come from security organizations (CSA/MAESTRO, OWASP ASI) and academic papers (GitInject), **not** from the AI code review vendors, who are the parties shipping the vulnerable agents.

### Cited Findings

**The concrete incident — best-documented case:**
- CSA research note on `claude-code-action`: the root cause was an **authorization bypass** in `checkWritePermissions`, which trusted any actor whose name ended in `[bot]`. An attacker using a malicious GitHub App installation token could open a crafted issue that passed the check. The injected prompt then drove the agent to read process environment variables, obtain an **OIDC token**, and exchange it for a privileged token with write access to the action's own repository — poisoning downstream repos pinned to floating tags. Assigned **CVE-2025-66032**, CVSS 4.0 **7.8** (the note also reports CVSS 3.1 as 8.7 for the same CVE — internally inconsistent) — [CSA Labs research note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-claude-code-github-action-prompt-injection/) `[FETCHED]`
- Secrets reachable from inside that agent session: `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY`, `GEMINI_API_KEY`, `GITHUB_TOKEN` (write), `GITHUB_COPILOT_API_TOKEN`, `GITHUB_PERSONAL_ACCESS_TOKEN`, `COPILOT_JOB_NONCE`, and the OIDC request variables `ACTIONS_ID_TOKEN_REQUEST_TOKEN` / `ACTIONS_ID_TOKEN_REQUEST_URL` which enable minting a privileged installation token. In the related "Clinejection" case, an npm publish token — [CSA Labs](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-claude-code-github-action-prompt-injection/) `[FETCHED]`
- Enumerated attack surfaces across incidents: **issue titles and bodies** (primary vector in the bypass chain and in Clinejection); **PR titles** interpolated directly into the prompt in the Claude Code Security Review case; **comments and HTML comments** — hidden HTML comments invisible to human reviewers in the rendered GitHub UI but parsed by the model (GitHub Copilot Agent), and "Trusted Content Sections" in issue comments (Gemini CLI); **PR descriptions, commit messages and code comments** listed as untrusted metadata. Notably, the CSA note **does not** treat the diff itself as an attack surface — [CSA Labs](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-claude-code-github-action-prompt-injection/) `[FETCHED]`
- Aikido reportedly identified at least five Fortune 500 companies with matching vulnerable configurations — [CSA Labs](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-claude-code-github-action-prompt-injection/) `[FETCHED]`
- A "Comment and Control" flaw class found several AI coding agents processing untrusted GitHub metadata — PR titles, issue bodies, HTML comments — as authoritative prompt content — [search synthesis of multiple incident write-ups] `[SNIPPET]`

**Recommended mitigations (CSA note — the most specific published set):**
- Upgrade `claude-code-action` to v1.0.94+ and `@anthropic-ai/claude-code` to v1.0.93+; remove `allowed_non_write_users: "*"` (treated as a critical misconfiguration)
- Pin third-party actions to **full commit SHAs**, not mutable tags
- Scope `id-token: write` and `contents: write` to only the workflows that need them
- **Run agents in workflows with no repository write credentials**, and require human approval (environment protection rules) before privileged workflows act on agent output
- Sanitize untrusted GitHub metadata before it reaches the model
- Restrict outbound network egress from CI runners
- **Separate the agent's reasoning layer — which only produces structured recommendations — from the credential-holding execution layer, which applies policy**
— all from [CSA Labs](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-claude-code-github-action-prompt-injection/) `[FETCHED]`

**Published frameworks:**
- **OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026**, released December 2025 by the OWASP GenAI Security Project, identifiers ASI01–ASI10: ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack, ASI02 Tool Misuse & Exploitation, ASI03 Identity & Privilege Abuse, ASI04 Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities, ASI05 Unexpected Code Execution (RCE), ASI06 Memory & Context Poisoning, ASI07 Insecure Inter-Agent Communication, ASI08 Cascading Failures, ASI09 Human-Agent Trust Exploitation, ASI10 Rogue Agents. Described as built by 100+ security experts with per-entry vulnerability patterns, attack scenarios and prevention guidance. Sources disagree on whether ASI10 is "rogue agents" or "behavioral misalignment" — confirm against genai.owasp.org — [Cycode](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/); [Teleport](https://goteleport.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/) `[SNIPPET]`
- **CSA MAESTRO** addresses untrusted content entering agent context (Layer 2), agent trust boundaries (Layer 4), and integration/deployment risks (Layer 6); a February 2026 CSA publication applies MAESTRO to CI/CD pipelines; the **AI Controls Matrix (AICM) v1.0** supply chain domain covers AI component provenance and integrity. The CSA note is explicit that **it does not provide a dedicated threat model specifically for PR content** — it points at MAESTRO instead — [CSA Labs](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-claude-code-github-action-prompt-injection/) `[FETCHED]`
- **GitInject: Real-World Prompt Injection Attacks in AI-Powered CI/CD Pipelines** (Isbarov, Suleymanov, Shumailov, Kantarcioglu; arXiv 2606.09935, cs.CR/cs.AI, code at github.com/ceferisbarov/GitInject). The paper's bookmarks reference example workflows for **Anthropic's Claude Code Action** (issue triage, deduplication, PR review, CI auto-fix, test-failure analysis, manual code analysis), **Google's Gemini CLI GitHub Action**, **OpenAI's Codex GitHub Action**, and the **Cline CLI GitHub integration**. Related work cited includes Greshake et al. on indirect prompt injection, AgentDojo, InjecAgent, Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta," the Aikido "PromptPwnd" write-up, the Cline post-mortem, and MITRE ATT&CK — [arXiv 2606.09935](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.09935) `[FETCHED metadata only — PDF text layer did not decode; threat model, success rates and mitigation effectiveness NOT retrieved]`
- Adversary model from a related study: "a remote, low-privileged adversary who cannot push to the repository's main branch, whose only lever is public input such as external fork PRs or issue comments" — and the key structural point, that an adversary who previously needed write access to a trusted repository "now needs only the ability to create an issue or pull request" — [search synthesis, incl. arXiv 2509.05372 on adversarial bug reports in LLM-based program repair](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.05372) `[SNIPPET]`
- Peer review is the analogous domain: "Publish to Perish: Prompt Injection Attacks on LLM-Assisted Peer Review" — [arXiv 2508.20863](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.20863) `[SNIPPET]`

**State of practice in the field (what teams actually do):**
- **Fence untrusted content as data.** Fence PR descriptions, commit messages and comments, and instruct the system prompt to treat them as data. One source is candid about the residual risk: even fenced, "injected text can't act, but it can change the take, the Findings or the Reading Order" — i.e. fencing prevents action, not influence `[SNIPPET]`
- **Separate trusted from untrusted message channels**, because reviewers were observed reading other conversations' messages as user input `[SNIPPET]`
- **Run offline injection payloads in CI** as an adversarial test suite. Documented caveat from one such project: "the tests script the model's replies, so whether a real model or a real reviewer is persuaded is not measured" — the test suite proves the plumbing, not the model's resistance `[SNIPPET]`
- **Require human approval**, which one paper treats as a distinct deployment mode that changes the threat model `[SNIPPET]`

### Inferences
- The CSA mitigation list independently arrives at two of the benchmark's design decisions: **no write credentials in the agent's workflow** and **separating the reasoning layer from the credential-holding execution layer**. So the *principle* is published; what I did not find published by any vendor is a per-product threat model naming the diff, title, body and comments as untrusted and asserting no file-backed secret is reachable in the review session.
- The CSA note explicitly declining to offer a PR-content-specific threat model, and pointing at MAESTRO's generic layers instead, is itself the finding: as of late 2026 there is **no canonical, rigorous, PR-specific threat model** in public. GitInject is the closest academic attempt and I could not read its contents.
- The vendors shipping AI PR review (CodeRabbit, Greptile, Qodo, Cursor Bugbot, Graphite Diamond) did not surface *any* published threat model in my searches. The security work is being done by Check Point/Palo Alto/CSA/OWASP/academia, on the *outside* of these products.
- The "lethal trifecta" framing (untrusted content + private data + external communication) is the operative mental model, and the only robust mitigation anyone proposes is removing one leg — which is what "no file-backed secret reachable in the review session" does.

### Gaps
- **GitInject's actual contents** — threat model, attack success rates, which agents were empirically tested vs. merely cited, mitigation effectiveness. The PDF text layer did not decode. This is the single highest-value gap; the arXiv HTML version or the GitHub repo would close it.
- No vendor-published threat model for AI PR review was found for any of the named code review products. I cannot rule out that one exists in security documentation I did not reach.
- Exact OWASP ASI10 naming needs confirmation against the primary genai.owasp.org document.
- Whether CVE-2025-66032's fix fully closes the metadata-as-instructions class, or only the `[bot]` authorization bypass, is not clear from the note.

## AI CODE REVIEW: documented problems, false-positive rates, and self-verification

### Takeaway
The documented problems are noise, speculation and a measurement vacuum: no independent, methodologically credible precision/recall benchmark exists, every public number comes from a vendor or a direct competitor, and they disagree by factors of 3–4x on the same tools. The most important finding for the benchmark: **exactly one shipping product executes code to demonstrate a bug before reporting it (Greptile TREX, public beta)**, and one more (Early's Release Guard) runs a non-executing verification stage. Neither is adversarial, neither emits a three-valued verdict, and neither is positioned as a blocking gate requiring reproduction.

- **The gating multiplier, which is the key governance insight:** behind a required status check, "every false positive stops a merge and pulls a human into the override," making the same FP rate far more costly than in an advisory comment stream — [pyor.review](https://pyor.review/blog/ai-review-alert-fatigue) `[SNIPPET]`
- Reviewer-fatigue mechanism: noise "doesn't just dilute review; it retrains the reviewer" — each dismissed false positive makes the next comment, including a real catch, get less attention — [dev.to/pyor](https://dev.to/pyor/alert-fatigue-comes-for-code-review-16kj) `[SNIPPET]`
- Practical mitigations cited: measure dismissal rate per bot; **run one AI reviewer, not several, because their false positives don't overlap while their true positives mostly do**; feed back "this flag was incorrect" — [pyor.review](https://pyor.review/blog/ai-review-alert-fatigue) `[SNIPPET]`

**Who verifies their own findings — the direct answer to the benchmark's central question:**
- **Greptile TREX (public beta)** — the only shipping product found that *executes* to demonstrate a bug. It "runs your branch in a sandbox to find bugs. it mocks api calls, clicks around the UI, and writes + runs unit tests." Greptile claims **~20% more bugs caught** than base Greptile in its own evals, and its founder states "most of the new bugs caught could not have been caught with more inference, they required code execution." Findings ship with artifacts — logs, screenshots, video — and **a passing run still gets a summary entry showing the change was exercised** — [Greptile TREX](https://www.greptile.com/trex); [Greptile blog](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex); [Daksh Gupta on X](https://x.com/dakshgup/status/2066536928716235254) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- **TREX's own documented failure of the naive design:** an earlier *standalone* test-writing agent "did not work well" — "the tests weren't relevant to what the user was trying to do," creating "unnecessary noise" and also missing edge cases. Greptile's fix was to **fold execution into the review agent so it shares context with the reviewer** — which is the opposite of a stripped, context-isolated verifier — [Greptile engineering blog](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- **CodeRabbit** runs each review in a sandbox with the repo cloned, executing "more than 50 linters, static analyzers and SAST tools" while agents explore the codebase, and describes "a Verification agent, with sandboxed execution." Whether that agent reproduces bugs by running them is **not established** by anything I retrieved — [cubic.dev comparison](https://www.cubic.dev/blog/coderabbit-vs-greptile) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Early "Release Guard"** describes a verification stage that checks candidate findings against code before posting inline comments — but **that stage reads code without building or running it** — [startearly.ai](https://www.startearly.ai/post/top-11-ai-code-review-tools/) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- **The pattern is documented in the open-pattern literature, not in products.** "Reproduce Before Report" (Part 3 of the Agent-Assisted Code Review series, agentpatterns.ai, CC BY 4.0, no author or date given) specifies a gate that "drops any reviewer finding the verifier cannot reproduce against actual code behavior." Design details: the verifier is a **fresh-context agent that receives the diff and the claim, never the reviewer's reasoning**, and "should ideally come from a different model family, because of same-family bias in LLM judges." Accepted reproduction forms: a triggering input, a code path showing the contradiction, or a `file:line` citation — **naming-based inference is not accepted**. Per-class strictness is configured in a `REVIEW.md` (e.g. data-flow claims must cite both source and sink lines) — [learn.agentpatterns.ai](https://learn.agentpatterns.ai/code-review/reproduce-before-report/) `[FETCHED]`
  - Where that pattern **differs from the benchmark**: outcomes are effectively **binary (reproduced, or silently dropped)** — no CONFIRMED / NOT REPRODUCED / UNVERIFIABLE labels; it does **not** describe a stripped environment or worktree copy, and does not say whether the verifier executes code; it does **not** state how an unrunnable check is labeled, and it **drops unreproduced findings silently** while warning against low-confidence flags. It routes reproduction-incapable classes (races, distributed failures, flakes) to a separate "unverified-suspicion" channel rather than dropping them — [learn.agentpatterns.ai](https://learn.agentpatterns.ai/code-review/reproduce-before-report/) `[FETCHED]`
- **"No proof, no report" as a configured discipline:** a Claude Code / Codex review skill requires the reviewer to prove each issue with a real failing scenario before reporting, and stays read-only until the user authorizes a fix — [photostructure.com](https://photostructure.com/coding/claude-code-review/) `[SNIPPET]`
- Forced reproduction traces as a noise reducer: requiring each finding to include a trace of how the bug happens reportedly cuts noise and gives the fixing agent a concrete target — [dev.to/shupf](https://dev.to/shupf/make-your-code-review-agent-write-down-how-the-bug-actually-happens-1g1) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Multi-agent verification research, and what it is NOT:** CodeX-Verify (arXiv:2511.16708, Shreshth Rajan) uses **four specialized agents each targeting different bug types** with low inter-agent correlation (ρ = 0.05–0.25); accuracy rises from **32.8% single-agent to 72.4% multi-agent** (a 39.7-point gain, with diminishing returns of +14.9/+13.5/+11.2 for agents 2–4); best two-agent combo (Correctness + Performance) hits 79.3%; 76.1% bug catch on 99 labeled samples, <200ms per sample on 300 real Claude Sonnet 4.5 patches. **Critically: there is no adversarial refuter** — the agents detect different problems, they do not challenge each other — and it works **"without test execution,"** i.e. static reasoning. The widely-cited "32.8% → 72.4%" figure is **accuracy, not precision**, and it is about verifying *code*, not verifying *review findings* — [arXiv 2511.16708](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16708) `[FETCHED abstract]`

### Inferences
- **The measurement vacuum is the headline.** Greptile's own benchmark shows a 38-point gap over CodeRabbit where Martian's leaderboard shows 0.1 points. Both cannot be right. Any report should state that **there is no credible public precision/recall figure for any AI code review product**, and that the reported numbers are marketing artifacts of divergent methodologies.
- The thread's observation that true positives overlap across tools while false positives don't is the strongest argument against buying multiple reviewers, and the strongest argument *for* verification: deduplicating noise is a verification problem, not an ensemble problem.
- Greptile's TREX is the closest product to the benchmark's refuter, and the difference is instructive and in the benchmark's favor on two axes: TREX is a **capability** (find more bugs by executing) not a **gate** (block findings that fail to reproduce), and Greptile deliberately **shares reviewer context with the executor** after the context-isolated version produced irrelevant tests. The benchmark's stripped-environment, fresh-context design is the *opposite* bet, and Greptile's blog is published evidence that the isolated-verifier approach has a known failure mode (irrelevant tests, missed edge cases) — this is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing.
- TREX's "a passing run still gets a summary entry showing the change was exercised" is the only product-side instance I found of positively reporting *what was exercised* rather than silently implying coverage. It is adjacent to honest skip-reporting but is not the same thing.
- Nobody found is shipping adversarial self-verification. The nearest shipping analogue is a *confirmatory* verifier (TREX executes to find bugs; Release Guard re-reads code to check candidates). A refuter whose success condition is disproving the finding is, on this evidence, not a product feature anywhere.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Martian leaderboard itself (the fetch returned only the page title), so its precision figures are snippet-level and its methodology and dataset are unknown. This is the most important verification gap in this section — it is the only semi-independent benchmark.
- CodeRabbit's "Verification agent" — whether it executes code or only re-reads it — is unresolved. Only CodeRabbit's docs would settle it.
- No independent study of reviewer fatigue in AI code review specifically exists; the evidence is anecdote plus analogies from SOC alerting (one 2023 Vectra figure of 83% false positives among SOC alerts) and radiology.
- The last ~65k characters of the HN thread were not read.
- Qodo's, Snyk's and Semgrep's AI features were not individually investigated beyond benchmark mentions; Snyk/Semgrep AI governance features are a genuine coverage gap in these notes.

## AGENT LIFECYCLE: risk tiering, proportional review, staged rollout, release gating

### Takeaway
Risk tiering and tier-proportional gating for agents exist as **published governance frameworks and platform policy guidance** (Microsoft's three-tier model, TrustX ARC, feature-flag playbooks), and staged rollout/rollback exist as **platform capability** in orchestration and agent-ops products. What I found no evidence of anywhere is a product that **derives** the risk tier mechanically from the artifact rather than accepting a human- or author-declared classification.

### Cited Findings
- **Microsoft's "Govern agents by risk"** uses three tiers; an agent starts in the tier that fits its behavior and **moves to a higher tier as its scope or autonomy grows**. The high-risk tier requires a security review and a responsible-AI assessment before release — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/agents/center-of-excellence/govern-agents-risk) `[SNIPPET]`
- **TrustX Agent Risk Classification Framework (ARC)** (Liu, Saxena, Asthana; arXiv 2607.09586, CC BY 4.0, cs.AI): a structured, repeatable instrument covering **seven types of agentic AI systems**, built on existing AI governance frameworks. Core is a **twelve-dimension scoring rubric** working alongside a "GPA + IAT" classification model and a **five-level autonomy framework**, producing a **three-tier governance output with mapped control recommendations**. Includes a **specialized extension for Coding Assistants**. Interactive version at arc.responsible.ai — [arXiv 2607.09586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09586) `[FETCHED abstract]`
  - **The abstract does not say whether tier scores are self-declared or mechanically derived**, nor what the per-tier gates are. It describes a scored process without specifying who assigns the inputs — [arXiv 2607.09586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09586) `[FETCHED abstract]`
- **Tier by action, not by agent** — a practitioner argument that the most useful shift is to tier on the *verb* (submit, send, delete, transfer), because that is where the risk sits — [Island.io](https://www.island.io/article/ai-agent-governance) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Staged gates for agents** from an arXiv survey: offline regression checks → simulation/sandbox stress tests → shadow or canary release with online monitoring, with the gates themselves tiered, which "reduces the chance that an agent 'wins on average' while failing under rare but foreseeable conditions" — [arXiv 2605.23989](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.23989) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Release gating tied to tier** in a feature-flag governance playbook: low-risk changes get normal review; high-risk changes (payments, permissions, customer-impacting automation) require "human approval, staged rollout, stop signal, audit evidence," with blast radius limited to internal users, beta accounts, a region, or a percentage rollout — [FeatBit](https://www.featbit.co/blogs/ai-generated-code-governance) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Staged rollout and rollback as product capability:** shadowing, canaries, weighted traffic, or workflow-specific rollout rather than immediate full deployment, with automatic or human rollback triggers — [Giga](https://giga.ai/news/ai-agent-versioning-rollbacks-and-release-governance) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Build-time vs. deploy-time separation:** initial risk tiers assigned at design time, then explicit approval workflows holding high-risk agents out of production until documentation and controls are in place — [Kovrr](https://www.kovrr.com/blog-post/ai-agent-governance-how-enterprises-should-approach-it) `[SNIPPET]` `[VENDOR]`
- **TADL lifecycle framework** ties deployment to staged rollout and "risk-proportionate capability restrictions" — [arXiv 2609.22712](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.22712) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Enterprise middleware governance features**, for comparison: Moveworks (internal IT/HR, quote-only six-figure pricing, SOC 2 Type 2 + ISO, FedRAMP, GDPR/CCPA; 8–16 week integration effort typical) emphasizes "decision boundaries, monitoring, and audit trails"; Sierra (customer-facing, outcome-based pricing, no published rate card) ships an Agent SDK, **GitHub Actions CI/CD and simulation testing**; Writer adds "orchestration, approvals, observability, and administrative controls" on enterprise plans — [Moveworks blog](https://www.moveworks.com/us/en/resources/blog/how-to-evaluate-agentic-ai-platform-enterprise-workflows) `[VENDOR]`; [cybernews Moveworks review](https://cybernews.com/ai-tools/moveworks-review-enterprise-ai-for-employee-support/); [francisokafor Sierra review](https://www.francisokafor.com/tools/sierra) `[SNIPPET]`
- **Durable execution / orchestration layer**, which is where staged rollout and recovery actually live: Temporal is positioned as most mature for long-running mission-critical workflows (cited at a $5B valuation and 9.1 trillion lifetime actions, with OpenAI and Block in production) at the cost of deterministic-workflow and event-history-replay complexity, and offers **Signals, Updates and Worker Versioning**. Inngest optimizes developer experience with HTTP-endpoint deployment, but **keys memoization on step names, so renaming a step orphans every in-flight run**. LangGraph is strongest when the agent is already a LangGraph graph; LangChain's own comparison claims agent-native streaming/memory/human-in-the-loop that Temporal lacks, while independent write-ups note **LangGraph's async checkpoint mode can lose the newest checkpoint on a hard crash** and that "LangGraph + Temporal (or Restate, or DBOS)" is the common production stack — [LangChain comparison](https://www.langchain.com/resources/langgraph-vs-temporal) `[VENDOR]`; [Cadenya](https://cadenya.com/other-content/durable-execution-for-ai-agents); [HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/durable-execution-for-ai-agents-langgraph-dbos-inngest-and-temporal-compared) `[SNIPPET]`
- **CrewAI** — core framework MIT-licensed; the enterprise platform is **now named CrewAI AMP**, with the paid solo/mid tier discontinued; the vendor page lists only a free Basic and a custom-priced Enterprise plan, so widely-cited $299/mo and $500/mo figures are likely stale. Enterprise features: managed deployment, scheduling, SOC 2 + SSO, no-code Studio, monitoring. **Four critical CVEs disclosed March–April 2026, fixed in v1.14.0 by removing the Code Interpreter tool** — [javisreview](https://javisreview.com/review/crewai-review-2026/) `[SNIPPET]`
- **LangGraph Platform** pricing is inconsistent across sources: one describes a free tier at 10K node executions/mo with Plus at ~$0.001/node and Enterprise on request; LangSmith Plus at $39/seat/mo. Enterprise custom-quoted with SLAs — [pooyagolchian](https://pooyagolchian.com/blog/crewai-vs-langgraph-autogen-comparison-2026/) `[SNIPPET]`

**On honest skip-reporting — the finding is that it is engineering discipline, not product:**
- Every instance I found of "a skip is never a pass" is an individual PR or issue in a small repository, not a product feature. Representative cases: a harness with no runner recorded every case as passed, reporting "4/4 and 100%" — the fix marks those skipped, computes pass rate only over cases that ran, and states plainly that nothing was measured ([pipeshub PR #3187](https://github.com/pipeshub-ai/pipeshub-ai/pull/3187)); a conformance report where "`conformant` is strict: every check ran and none failed or errored. A single skip makes it false," and the report lists the unverified check IDs ([HarnessRouter PR #8](https://github.com/HarnessRouter/harnessrouter/pull/8)); a CLI where a check that wanted to run and could not "reports status: 'skip' with a skipReason, is not counted in summary.passed, and forces exit 1," while an explicit operator `--skip` stays an acknowledged exit-0 non-run ([harness-engineering PR #2207](https://github.com/Intense-Visions/harness-engineering/pull/2207)); a harness that reads the Playwright JSON report post-run and fails the shard when a skip looks environment-related rather than trusting the exit code, and never trusts an unreadable report as a pass ([iHYPE PR #949](https://github.com/iHYPE-org/ihype/pull/949)); a harness that exited 0 saying "all stages passed or skipped" ([cod4-decomp issue #375](https://github.com/jeiang/cod4-decomp/issues/375)). All `[SNIPPET]`
- The recurring principles across these: a zero-observation gate is "unknown," not a pass; skips counted separately from passes; reports must name what did not run; exit code must reflect unexecuted checks unless the skip was explicitly requested.

### Inferences
- The lifecycle capability is **split across layers and sold by nobody as a whole**: tiering lives in governance frameworks (Microsoft, ARC, Kovrr), gating lives in feature-flag and CI tooling, staged rollout lives in durable-execution and agent-ops platforms, and simulation testing lives in a few middleware products (Sierra). An organization wanting risk-tiered, proportional, gated agent release in 2026 assembles it.
- Microsoft's model is the closest to a shipping "risk tier → required review" policy, and it is **declarative**: an agent "starts in the tier that fits its behavior." That is author-declared, which is precisely the weakness the benchmark's mechanical computation addresses.
- The near-total absence of honest skip-reporting as a *product* feature, combined with how many individual repos are fixing this bug by hand, suggests it is a real and widespread defect that no vendor treats as a selling point. The pipeshub case ("4/4 and 100%" with no runner) is the canonical failure.

### Gaps
- **Whether TrustX ARC's twelve-dimension rubric is scored mechanically or by a human assessor is unresolved** — the abstract does not say and the PDF text layer did not decode. arc.responsible.ai (the interactive version) would settle it and would be the single best place to check whether anyone computes tiers from artifact properties.
- No product was found that computes a risk tier from a code diff. I cannot prove none exists; I can say none surfaced across searches of agent governance, release gating, and AI code review.
- CrewAI AMP and LangGraph Platform enterprise pricing are custom-quoted; no reliable public figures.
- I did not investigate Stack AI or Relevance AI at all — coverage gap.
- Snyk and Semgrep AI-era governance features were not researched.

## WHAT THESE PRODUCTS DO THAT THE BENCHMARK DOES NOT

Honest list, so the comparison isn't one-sided:

1. **Execute the branch to find bugs, not just to verify claims.** Greptile TREX installs dependencies, starts services, mocks API calls, drives the UI, and writes and runs unit tests — and reports ~20% more bugs caught, with its founder stating most of those "required code execution" and could not be reached by more inference. A refuter that only *disproves* candidate findings never generates that class of finding in the first place. [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex)

## WHAT THE BENCHMARK DOES THAT THESE PRODUCTS DO NOT

1. **Adversarial self-verification as a gate.** Nothing found inverts the verifier's objective to *disproving* the finding. TREX executes to *find* bugs; Early's Release Guard re-reads code to *confirm* candidates; CodeX-Verify ensembles *detectors*. A refuter whose success condition is NOT REPRODUCED has no shipping analogue.
2. **Three-valued verdicts with UNVERIFIABLE as a first-class outcome.** The open "Reproduce Before Report" pattern — the closest published design — is explicitly binary and **drops unreproduced findings silently**. Distinguishing "I disproved this" from "I could not test this" is not done anywhere I found.
3. **Blocking findings require a reproduction, as a hard rule.** Published as a configured discipline in individual setups ([photostructure](https://photostructure.com/coding/claude-code-review/), [agentpatterns](https://learn.agentpatterns.ai/code-review/reproduce-before-report/)) but not as a product rule in any commercial reviewer. The gating-multiplier argument makes this the highest-leverage governance decision available. [pyor.review](https://pyor.review/blog/ai-review-alert-fatigue)
4. **Honest skip-reporting as policy.** Not a feature of any commercial product found. TREX's "summary entry showing the change was exercised" is the only adjacent thing in a product.
5. **Risk tier computed mechanically from changed file paths, with max(declared, computed).** Every tiering scheme found is declarative or assessor-scored. The non-downgradable property — the author cannot understate their own blast radius — did not appear anywhere.
6. **An explicit prohibition on LLM self-review loops.** Vendors sell more LLM passes as the fix for LLM noise. The one pattern source that engages with this recommends a *different model family* to dodge same-family judge bias, not a ban. [agentpatterns](https://learn.agentpatterns.ai/code-review/reproduce-before-report/)
7. **A per-product threat model treating diff/title/body/comments as untrusted with no file-backed secret reachable in the review session.** The *principles* are published by CSA (no write credentials in the agent workflow; separate reasoning from the credential-holding layer) — but no AI code review vendor publishes a threat model. [CSA Labs](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-claude-code-github-action-prompt-injection/)
8. **Tokenizer-level output enforcement in production.** The only comparable mechanism found is PCC-SQL's per-token logits mask, which is a research prototype. [arXiv 2607.12341](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.12341)

## VERDICT ON THE SIX BENCHMARK-SPECIFIC QUESTIONS

**1. Does ANY shipping product do adversarial self-verification of its own findings?**
**No — the novelty claim survives, but narrow it.** No product found uses a refuter whose objective is to disprove a candidate finding. But **the reproduce-before-report *pattern* is published prior art** ([learn.agentpatterns.ai](https://learn.agentpatterns.ai/code-review/reproduce-before-report/), CC BY 4.0), including the fresh-context verifier that never sees the reviewer's reasoning and the cross-model-family recommendation. **Greptile TREX ships sandboxed code execution** to substantiate findings. Claim the *adversarial framing*, the *three-valued verdict*, and the *gate* — not "verification before reporting," which is both published and partly shipping. **Also note the counter-evidence:** Greptile's engineering blog documents that a *context-isolated* execution agent produced irrelevant tests and missed edge cases, and that they fixed it by sharing reviewer context. The stripped-environment design has a vendor-documented failure mode worth testing against.

**2. How common is honest skip-reporting vs. silently treating unavailable checks as passing?**
**Rare to nonexistent as a product feature; the silent-pass bug is demonstrably common.** Every example found is a hand-rolled fix in an individual repo — a harness reporting "4/4 and 100%" with no runner ([pipeshub #3187](https://github.com/pipeshub-ai/pipeshub-ai/pull/3187)), exit 0 on "passed or skipped" ([cod4-decomp #375](https://github.com/jeiang/cod4-decomp/issues/375)), green checks over a single skipped test. No commercial eval or review platform markets strict skip semantics. The benchmark's position is **defensible and, on this evidence, unusually rigorous**.

**3. Does any product compute risk from the diff rather than accepting a self-declared label?**
**None found.** Microsoft's three-tier model is declarative ("an agent starts in the tier that fits its behavior"); TrustX ARC is a twelve-dimension *scored* rubric but the abstract does not say who supplies the scores; feature-flag playbooks tier by change category assigned at design time. **Caveat: I could not read ARC's body text, and arc.responsible.ai is unchecked — that is the one place a mechanically-derived tier might already exist.** Verify before publishing the novelty claim. The `max(declared, computed)` anti-downgrade property appeared nowhere.

**4. Who else has published an equivalent threat model (PR content as untrusted, no reachable file-backed secret)?**
**Nobody at the product level; the principles are published at the security-research level.** CSA's note on CVE-2025-66032 independently recommends running agents in workflows with no repository write credentials and separating the reasoning layer from the credential-holding execution layer — which is functionally the same conclusion. OWASP ASI 2026 (ASI01 Goal Hijack, ASI03 Identity & Privilege Abuse, ASI04 Supply Chain) and CSA MAESTRO Layers 2/4/6 cover the category. **The CSA note explicitly declines to offer a PR-content-specific threat model**, which confirms the gap. GitInject (arXiv 2606.09935) is the nearest academic work and I could not read it — **check it before claiming no equivalent exists.** The claim to make: no *vendor* has published one; the benchmark's threat model is consistent with CSA's recommendations and not contradicted by them.

**5. What do guardrail products actually use — prompt, classifier, or deterministic?**
**Overwhelmingly classifier, with schema/regex as the deterministic veneer and flow-matching-over-a-vector-index in NeMo's case.** Bedrock's PII filter self-describes as "a probabilistic machine learning (ML) based solution"; Datadog uses regex scrubbing on output; Guardrails AI validators are "fast and deterministic" but "logically shallow." **Tokenizer-level enforcement is not a product** — the only instance found is PCC-SQL's per-token logits mask (research, 0% leakage on Spider-CU, authors' own benchmark). **So the benchmark's tokenizer approach is genuinely ahead of the product market** — but the stronger argument in 2026 is architectural, not lexical: the literature says consequential enforcement belongs in the DB grant / semantic layer / tool layer, where the agent cannot route around it. If the benchmark's tokenizer is the *last* line before egress rather than one layer among several, the EchoGram-class critique (classifier verdicts flipped by short token sequences) and the "redact before you log" failure mode both still apply in spirit.

**6. Is anyone arguing for deterministic checks instead of LLM-as-judge?**
**Yes, and it is the mainstream practitioner position, not a contrarian one.** Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's FAQ (updated 2026-09-18) says to use code-based evals "when a deterministic rule can identify the failure" and to favor assertions over LLM judges, reserving judges for failures requiring human judgment and only after TPR/TNR validation against 100–200 human labels per failure mode. The 2026 academic literature reinforces it from the other side: judges can be highly reliable and still invalid (κ deflation of 33–41 points; test–retest >0.95 alongside severe position bias; rankings shifting up to 14 positions). **The benchmark's "deterministic verification only, LLM self-review loops forbidden" is squarely aligned with the best practitioner guidance — but it is stricter than that guidance, which permits validated judges for irreducibly subjective failures.** If the benchmark has any such failure classes, forbidding judges outright means those go unevaluated rather than evaluated imperfectly. That is the honest tradeoff to flag.

# ROUND 2 — GAP CLOSURE

Three targeted retrievals, all primary-source `[FETCHED]`. Two of the three were the gaps flagged as potentially falsifying a headline novelty claim. **Net result: one claim survives and is strengthened, one claim must be downgraded from "novel" to "consistent with published research," and the Greptile counter-evidence turns out to be about a different activity than ours — but it exposes a real, different tradeoff that should be stated.**

## Gap 1 — TrustX ARC: is the risk tier derived from the artifact or self-declared?

### Takeaway
**Self-declared. The novelty claim survives, and ARC's own limitations section strengthens it.** ARC is a human self-assessment rubric. It derives nothing from code, diffs, file paths or telemetry, and it has no anti-downgrade precedence rule. Its "critical dimension rule" is a conceptual cousin of `max(declared, computed)` but operates entirely *within* self-reported scores, so it is a floor on a self-report, not a check against a mechanical computation.

### Cited Findings
- **Scoring is a user selection on a 1–3 scale.** "Each dimension is scored via a point scale of (1) Low, (2) Medium, and (3) High" (§3.2.4). The framework "allow[s] the user to select the most appropriate autonomy level" (§3.2.3) — [arXiv 2607.09586v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09586v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **The paper never describes deriving scores from artifacts** — no code, diff, file-path or telemetry input anywhere. The word "questionnaire" is not used, but the mechanism is self-assessment — [arXiv 2607.09586v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09586v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **The authors concede the exact weakness our mechanical derivation addresses.** §6.3 acknowledges "scoring subjectivity" and that "differences in user interpretation can lead to varying results" — [arXiv 2607.09586v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09586v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **Aggregation:** "The framework then takes a sum of all risk dimension scores, before averaging them across the number of available risk dimensions" (§3.2.4). The highest individual score is retained separately for the critical-dimension rule — [arXiv 2607.09586v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09586v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **Tier rules (§3.3):** Tier 1 requires all dimensions at 1 *and* average below 1.5. Tier 2 if max dimension is 2 *or* average ≥ 1.5. Tier 3 if "any dimension scores a (3) High, or if the agent's autonomy level is L5." Multi-agent systems **inherit the highest tier among their agents** — [arXiv 2607.09586v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09586v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **No `max(declared, computed)` and no anti-downgrade rule.** The nearest mechanism is the critical-dimension rule, which ensures "a high-scoring risk dimension or risk component is not averaged away by our formulas." That is a floor on the dimension side only. Reassessment triggers are mentioned; no precedence or override rules are given — [arXiv 2607.09586v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09586v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **The Coding Assistants extension (§4, §5.7) is weaker than it first appears.** It replaces GPA+IAT with 20 capabilities that "do not feed the final calculation but inform the user's other selections." It defines four deployment models M1–M4 with expected tiers 1 / 2 / 2–3 / 3, but states this classification "does not feed into the final risk calculation" and serves only as a check on the autonomy selection. It adds 20 coding-specific risk factors in three categories — supply chain and artifact integrity, agent manipulation and workflow hijacking, access and privilege abuse — which the user selects manually; these replace the generic additional factors, and §3.2.5 says those do not contribute to the calculation — [arXiv 2607.09586v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09586v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **The paper's own worked example is internally inconsistent:** the M1 example is expected to map to Tier 1 but lands at Tier 2 because Persistence and Data Sensitivity score 2 (§5.7) — [arXiv 2607.09586v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09586v1) `[FETCHED]`

### Inferences
- **Safe to put in a deck:** no published framework or product found computes agent risk from the artifact. ARC was the most likely candidate and it is explicitly a self-assessment whose authors name scoring subjectivity as a limitation.
- The useful rhetorical move is *not* "nobody has risk tiers" — ARC, Microsoft and Kovrr all have tiers. It is "every published tiering scheme scores a self-report, and at least one of them names that as its own weakness."
- ARC's multi-agent inheritance rule ("highest tier among their agents") is directionally the same instinct as `max()` — worth citing as evidence the principle is recognized, just never applied against a mechanically computed value.
- The Coding Assistants extension is the most relevant prior art to a code-review agent, and the finding is that **its 20 coding-specific risk factors (including "agent manipulation and workflow hijacking" and "access and privilege abuse") are collected but do not affect the tier.** That is a substantive design gap, not a nuance.

### Gaps
- **arc.responsible.ai could not be read.** The page returned only its title, "Risk Classifier - Responsible AI Institute" — it is a client-rendered JS application that WebFetch cannot execute. I therefore cannot rule out that the interactive tool ingests something the paper does not describe. **This is an explicit could-not-retrieve, not an inferred answer.** Given §3.2.3 and §6.3, a mechanically-derived interactive version would contradict the paper's own text, so the risk is low — but if this claim is going in a deck, someone should open that URL in a browser and confirm.

## Gap 2 — GitInject (arXiv 2606.09935): threat model, agents tested, success rates, defense posture

### Takeaway
**The threat-model novelty claim must be downgraded.** GitInject's system model explicitly labels "PR titles, bodies, issue text, code under review" as untrusted external content, so the untrusted-input half of our posture is published. The no-reachable-secret half is *not* stated as a principle, but the paper's recommendations converge on it. More importantly, GitInject surfaces **two findings that make our claim's phrasing too narrow**: provider config files added in the PR branch are loaded at *operator* trust level, and a secret was recovered from the **parent process**, not from a file.

### Cited Findings

**Threat model — closely matches ours:**
- "A remote, low-privileged adversary who can open a PR from a fork, post issue comments, or add files to a PR branch, but cannot push to main or access secrets." The agent is the privileged actor: it holds a `GITHUB_TOKEN` with write access, can run shell commands on the runner, and can post comments — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`
- The structural framing: these agents "must read content the repository owner doesn't control," and the core concern is that **trusted operator instructions and untrusted external content share the same context window** — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`

**Agents empirically tested — four providers, named models:**
- **Anthropic (Claude):** claude-sonnet-4-5, claude-haiku-4-5, claude-opus-4-7
- **OpenAI (Codex):** gpt-4o-mini, gpt-5, gpt-5.4
- **Google (Gemini):** gemini-2.5-flash, gemini-3-flash, gemini-3.1-pro-preview
- **Cline:** cline-assistant workflow
— [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`

**Attack success rates:**
- Table 2, by MITRE category: **15 of 22 attempted scenarios confirmed** — Credential Access 5/9, Defense Evasion 5/6, Discovery 1/3, Impact 4/4 (tally of table cells by the fetching reader, not a figure stated in prose — treat as approximate) — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`
- Table 3, **PR/issue body injection: 2 of 2 on gpt-4o-mini, 0 on every other model** including all Claude and Gemini models — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`
- Table 3, **config-file injection succeeded much more broadly:** sonnet-4-5, haiku-4-5, gpt-4o-mini, gpt-5, gemini-2.5-flash, gemini-3-flash and gemini-3.1-pro-preview all showed successes. Only opus-4-7 and gpt-5.4 were 0 of 2 — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **Internal inconsistency in the paper:** the text says PR-body exfiltration succeeded against gemini-assistant, but Table 3 lists 0/4 for Gemini models on PR/issue body injection. The paper does not reconcile this — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`

**Attack surfaces — including one we should check our own coverage against:**
- PR body and issue body (treated at **user-turn** trust level)
- Issue comments — the cline-assistant triggers on "@cline" comments **with no author-association check**
- **Provider config files added in the PR branch — `CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `GEMINI.md` — loaded as operator-level instructions.** This is the highest-success vector in the paper and it is a *trust-level escalation*, not merely an injection channel
- Code under review — a misleading comment in a source file was used alongside a `CLAUDE.md` scope restriction to manipulate approval
- **The paper does not separately test PR titles or diffs as injection channels**, though it lists PR titles and code under review among the untrusted inputs
— [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`

**Defense posture — what the paper does and does not say:**
- **It does label the inputs untrusted.** The system model explicitly marks "PR titles, bodies, issue text, code under review" as untrusted external content. **But it does not frame this as a design principle** such as "treat all PR content as data." Its argument is that model-side alignment cannot reliably separate these inputs. It treats PR-branch config files as a trust-boundary failure but **does not explicitly say those files should be treated as untrusted too** — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **It does not state a secrets-free-session posture.** Its recommendations reduce secret exposure rather than eliminating reachability — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`
- Recommended defenses, in the paper's own priority order:
  - **`persist-credentials: false` on `actions/checkout`**, which stops the token being written to `.git/config` — called the **highest-priority, lowest-cost fix**, but explicitly noted as not covering other credential theft
  - **Tool restriction** — block shell commands like `grep`, `cat`, `tr` that exfiltration requires. Described as **the broadest single defense**, at a utility cost
  - **Author-association filtering** to limit who can trigger the agent
  - **Human review of AI-approved changes, or advisory-only agent permissions**, for judgment manipulation — because "no cheap workflow fix exists"
  - **Content filtering before invoking the agent**, for denial-of-wallet
  - **Stated minimum baseline for PR review workflows open to outside contributors: `persist-credentials: false` plus tool restriction**
— [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`
- **The finding that most directly complicates our phrasing:** for `GEMINI_API_KEY`, "the key was recovered from the parent process," and **only removing `tr` and `grep` from the allowed tools was effective.** Removing file-backed secrets would not have stopped this — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`
- Platform mechanics worth knowing: secrets are unavailable to fork PRs under `pull_request`, and `pull_request_target` is required for automated review that uses secrets — i.e. the standard way teams enable AI review on external contributions is precisely the configuration that reintroduces secret reachability — [arXiv 2606.09935v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09935v1) `[FETCHED]`

### Inferences
- **Claim change required.** "We publish a threat model nobody else has" is not defensible. The accurate claim is: *"Our threat model is consistent with, and partially anticipated by, GitInject (arXiv 2606.09935) and CSA's CVE-2025-66032 note. What remains unpublished by any vendor is a per-product threat model; what remains unpublished by anyone is the no-reachable-secret posture stated as a design rule rather than a set of mitigations."* That is still a real differentiator and it is honest.
- **Two concrete action items for our own design, which are the most valuable output of this round:**
  1. **Provider config files in the PR branch (`CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `GEMINI.md`) are loaded at operator trust level and were the highest-success vector — succeeding against 7 of 9 tested models, including sonnet-4-5 and haiku-4-5, where PR-body injection succeeded against only gpt-4o-mini.** If our review session reads repo-root agent config from the worktree copy, the untrusted-data boundary has a hole that is empirically far more exploitable than the channel we named. Our threat model names diff, title, body and comments — it does not name config files.
  2. **"No file-backed secret reachable" is too narrow.** A key was recovered from the **parent process**. The posture needs to be reachability-based (no secret recoverable from the session by any means, including process environment and parent-process memory), with tool restriction as the enforcing mechanism — which is exactly what GitInject found to be the only effective control in that case.
- The success-rate pattern is a useful argument *for* deterministic architecture over model choice: PR-body injection largely failed against frontier models (0/2 on most), while operator-trust-level config injection succeeded against nearly all of them. Model capability did not protect against a **trust-boundary** error. That is a strong, citable case that the defense must be architectural.
- GitInject's "advisory-only agent permissions" recommendation is independent third-party support for a review agent that produces findings and holds no write capability.

### Gaps
- The final ~29k characters of the HTML were not read, so the paper's limitations section and any additional defense evaluation are unretrieved.
- Table 2's 15/22 figure is a cell tally by the fetching reader, not a number the paper states in prose. Verify against the table before quoting it.
- The paper's own PR-body-vs-Table-3 inconsistency for Gemini is unresolved and should not be papered over if cited.

## Gap 3 (lower priority) — Greptile's context-isolation failure: generation or reproduction?

### Takeaway
**It was test GENERATION, not finding REPRODUCTION — so it does not falsify our stripped-environment bet.** But the *second*, unreported failure mode does create a genuine tradeoff: Greptile's fix deliberately gives each per-issue subagent what the orchestrator already found, which is the direct opposite of "the refuter never sees the reviewer's reasoning." The defensible position is that we are paying a compute and scoping cost to buy independence, and should say so rather than claim no cost.

### Cited Findings
- **The earlier design:** TREX "began as a separate product, a standalone agent that generated and ran tests" — [Greptile engineering blog](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Failure mode 1 — irrelevant generation:** "Generating tests wasn't the same activity as finding bugs." Generated tests often didn't match what the user was trying to do, adding noise and missing edge cases — [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Failure mode 2 — duplicated exploration:** the separate agents had no shared knowledge and often explored "the same parts of the codebase twice," wasting compute — [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`
- **A third design also failed:** merging the two into one agent overloaded it — a single agent handling the full review had too much context to manage — [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Explicitly not a reproduction failure:** the article does not describe a reproduction step that failed to confirm candidate findings — [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`
- **The actual fix — an orchestrator with per-issue subagents:** the main Greptile reviewer acts as orchestrator; it "reads the diff, identifies issues worth investigating, and spins up a dedicated TREX agent per issue." **Each subagent has its own context window, inherits what the orchestrator already found, and is scoped to one problem** — [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Sandbox:** a disposable, isolated compute instance started fresh and discarded after the run; can run real services with real dependencies, not just mocked unit tests; uses reusable base images and per-repository snapshots for setup speed; **each review fetches the exact PR commits and rotates credentials before execution** — [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Artifacts:** per finding, a multi-modal artifact set rather than a bullet summary — screenshots, logs, API traces, execution scripts, and video for animation changes — "meant to let a reviewer or downstream agent verify exactly what ran" — [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`
- **Passing-run reporting, confirmed from the primary source:** a run where a feature works is not a bug report; instead it "goes in the summary as proof the change was actually tested," and "not every run needs to find a problem to be useful" — [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/blog/trex-code-execution) `[FETCHED]` `[VENDOR]`

### Inferences
- **The distinction the coordinator asked about holds, and it matters.** Greptile's isolated agent failed at *inventing tests without knowing what the change was for*. A refuter that is handed a specific, already-articulated claim and asked to disprove it is not performing that activity and does not inherit that failure. Our architecture is not contradicted by this post.
- **But there is a real tradeoff, and it is the second failure mode.** Greptile converged on subagents that *inherit the orchestrator's findings* and are *scoped to one problem*. We match the per-issue scoping. We deliberately reject the inheritance. The honest statement: independence costs compute (re-exploration) and costs the refuter the orchestrator's context, in exchange for eliminating the confirmation bias that the agentpatterns source identifies as same-family judge bias. Greptile optimized for cost and relevance; we optimize for independence. Both are coherent; ours is more expensive.
- **Greptile's "summary as proof the change was actually tested" is now confirmed from the primary source** and stands as the one product-side analogue to positive reporting of what was exercised. It is still not honest *skip*-reporting — it reports what ran, not what could not run — but it is closer than anything else found, and a report should not claim no product does anything in this area.
- **Credential rotation before execution** is a Greptile practice worth noting alongside GitInject's findings: it is a reachability mitigation in a shipping product, applied in the sandbox rather than as a session-level posture.

### Gaps
- Entirely vendor-authored. No independent verification of the failure narrative, the orchestrator design, or TREX's claimed ~20% improvement.
- The post does not quantify either failure mode (how many irrelevant tests, how much duplicated exploration), so the magnitude of the tradeoff we are accepting is unknown.
